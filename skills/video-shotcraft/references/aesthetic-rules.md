# Aesthetic Rules (Case-Precedent Style)

Source: precedents distilled from rework on several promo films (the finished-film template is in `template/`).

**How to use**:
- Each entry = a rule (one actionable sentence) + a precedent (the user's own words / how the rework went) + a self-check question. All three elements are required.
- At pipeline.md stage 7 (acceptance), **go through the entries one by one** and output a self-check report in the format `number ✓` or `number ✗(location)` (location = shot name / frame number).
- A rule may be broken deliberately (when the style needs it), but every violation must be written into that project's documentation (stating which rule was broken and why).
- Numbering scheme: R (rhythm) / Q (finish, camera work and composition) / S (sound) / C (copy) / P (process). Numbers are never renumbered once published; new entries are only appended.

## Contents

- Rhythm (R)
- Finish, camera work and composition (Q)
- Sound (S)
- Copy (C)
- Process (P)

## Rhythm (R)

### R1. After key information lands it must breathe; the brand wordmark holds a full 1 second
Rule: after an information-carrying element (a title card / number / the full view of a grid) lands, it stays still for ≥1s before the cut; the breathing pause goes first to the brand's memory point (the moment the wordmark lands), not to ordinary content cards.
Precedent: the user: "after the first title is typed out, stay for one second", "it's not the first title card, I mean the animation where AI field lab appears at the opening and the end; extend the hold by 1 second after it appears". The pause was first put on the wrong object, reverted and redone; the final version holds the wordmark for a full 1 second at both the opening and the end.
Self-check: does every "frame the audience should remember" have a complete still moment? Was the pause given to the brand / key information, or casually given to an ordinary element?

### R2. A sense of speed comes from acceleration, not constant fast speed; group motion carries a physical metaphor, and the ending leaves a 0.5s pause
Rule: fly-ins and scrolls always use non-linear easing + non-uniform staggering — constant-speed motion reads as a cheap slideshow; when many elements of the same kind enter, use a hard accelerating beat ("faster and faster"), ideally tied to a physical metaphor (such as dealing a deck of cards), and once the board is full hold still for 0.5s before the next beat.
Precedent: the user: "when the cards keep stacking downward they should get faster and faster, the scroll speed should also get faster and faster, and pause for 0.5 seconds at the end", "the speed at the end is still not fast enough... the cards automatically fly to their positions in order, settling faster and faster". It was first built as an accelerating scroll + 0.5s pause, then changed to a card-dealing hard acceleration — an abstract flood failed to converge in five rounds, and switching to the dealing metaphor passed in one round.
Self-check: is any element moving in a constant-speed straight line? Does a batch entrance get faster and faster, and does it end with a still breath?

### R3. Better slow than fast: slow the first draft down one notch by default, the main action arc ≥3s, interactive demos at real human operating speed
Rule: the opening's main action (focus → hover → settle as one complete arc) takes at least 3 seconds; simulated real interaction (typing, filtering, clicking) runs at the speed a person actually operates, so the audience can follow along and do it themselves; when laying out the timeline, reserve a hold/rest frame budget — the first version is almost always too fast.
Precedent: all six independent pieces of feedback on the film pointed to "slow down / stay longer", and there was never a single reverse "too slow". The user: "the first shot is still too fast, slow it down to 3 seconds", "the process of entering the search box, typing, and then filtering out the cards is too fast, slow it down a bit". The opening was slowed several times to a 3s macro opening, and the interaction segment was changed to an unhurried type-and-filter.
Self-check: watch each shot as if for the first time: what couldn't you see clearly? Could you follow the interaction segment and do it yourself?

### R4. Beat hits constrain timing, not amplitude: whole-frame beat impacts ≤3 in the whole film, by default only the element layer moves
Rule: in a strong-rhythm BGM film, "cuts land on the beat" does not mean "hit the frame on every beat" — impacts that act on the whole frame / camera layer (whole-frame scale pump / screen shake / flash frame / negative frame) are managed like big slams: ≤3 in the whole film, pinned to the strongest-hit list in music-beat-sync §2b, with adjacent ones ≥16 beats apart; on all other beat points the motion acts only on the hero element layer; beat-pump types hit consecutively ≤4 beats at a time and ≤1 time in the whole film. The kick hit table is a candidate pool, not a trigger. **The counting unit is one use of a technique / set-piece, not the number of internal sub-events**: one application of one card (paparazzi triple flash, trailer-bumper triple cut) counts as one slot; the burst structure marked on the card itself and the whole-film cap on the number of uses still apply, and are not counted frame by frame again under this rule.
Precedent: user feedback (2026-08-22): "if you add music, the shots will shake with the rhythm, which hurts the viewing experience" — the root cause was executing music-beat-sync's kick→impact mapping as a whole-frame pump on every beat: a strong-kick track has a kick on almost every beat, and being "slammed" on every beat stacked on the camera layer becomes picture shake at beat frequency. Same family as Q3: Q3 bans noise-type shake (handheld), this rule bans beat-type shake.
Self-check: in a high-energy section take any 8 consecutive beats and play back frame by frame: does the whole-frame scale / position pulse on every beat? Count the impacts acting on the whole frame / camera layer across the film: more than 3? Does each one match the strongest-hit list?

## Finish, camera work and composition (Q)

### Q1. Recreating an existing page must use a real screenshot; hand-built UI is limited to non-recreation scenes and must meet the quality / expression bar; data is handled according to its risk level
Rule: for any shot that shows an existing page of the product, the first step is to capture the real page with a headless browser (full-page texture + element-level cutouts + a coordinates json), and not to hand-build a simulated texture to recreate it; non-recreation scenes (abstract opening / brand section / standalone showcase components that do not exist on the page) may use hand-built UI, but quality (publication-grade finish, held to the Q10 standard) and clarity of expression (the audience instantly understands what it is) are hard thresholds — if they are not met, go back to the screenshot route. Public demo data may be kept only after the product brief explicitly confirms it; customer, personal, internal, secret, live or other sensitive content must first be fictionalised, redacted or frozen. The same standard applies to hand-built material.
Precedent: the user: "what I want is the real page, with animation added", "don't use real data, make a fake one, and don't mention customer names". The shot was rebuilt using the real dashboard page, and the previous afternoon's hand-built texture recreation was scrapped entirely. Revision (2026-07-13 user ruling): "it doesn't have to all come from real page screenshots, you can hand-build UI components, as long as the quality and the clarity of expression are guaranteed" — the precedent's constraint is narrowed to "do not hand-build recreations of existing pages".
Self-check: for each piece of UI material: is it recreating an existing page? If so it must come from a screenshot; can the finish and expression of a hand-built piece withstand Q10-level scrutiny? Does the source data conform to the confirmed data standard, and contain no unauthorised customer, personal, internal or secret information?

### Q2. UI textures in 3D perspective are rasterized at native size and then scaled down; if text is blurry, check the texture-resolution chain first
Rule: UI textures in a 3D scene are rasterized at 2–4× the display size and downsampled in 3D (enlarge with layout-level CSS zoom, not transform scale); if text is blurry, check the rasterization path first — do not touch the camera/DoF first.
Precedent: the user hit it three rounds in a row: "the cards have clearly become blurry", "still not sharp enough... there are pixel blocks", "the text clarity of the first shot is still not solved". Adjusting DoF first treated the symptom and had no effect; changing to rasterizing at native size gave true 4x sharpness, and finally extending CSS zoom to all 3D page layers fixed it at the root.
Self-check: take frame screenshots of zoom-in / perspective shots and look at the text edges: are there pixel blocks? Is the texture source resolution ≥ 2× the display size?

Technique expansion (the original hires-rasterize-3d-text technique card was distilled into this entry; reference implementation template/src/aifl/live/PageCam.tsx):
- **Root cause**: Chromium rasterizes 3D compositing layers at a 1920 layout width and then enlarges them on the GPU — content that is downsampled first and enlarged afterwards is bound to be blurry, regardless of camera / depth-of-field parameters.
- **Solution**: the enlargement does not go through `transform: scale(zoom)` but through the CSS `zoom` property (layout-level scaling), so Chromium rasterizes at the enlarged device size and downsamples from a high-definition source. Coordinates must be converted as `Tx = 960/zoom − cx` to stay consistent with the framing in flat mode (PageCam.tsx:100-114 has the full derivation); skipping the conversion makes the focal point drift.
- **Supporting measures**: full page at 2x (deviceScaleFactor: 2), the hero element captured separately at 4x as an element-level screenshot, and a 6f cross-fade during the push-in to cover the low-resolution page texture (if the fade is too long you will see two misaligned layers).
- **Troubleshooting order**: blurry text → first check the texture source resolution chain (screenshot scale → rasterization path → scaling method), and only last consider the camera. DoF only provides atmosphere and is never the solution to sharpness (shown to be ineffective in practice). Verify by cropping and enlarging frame-by-frame screenshots to look at the text edges; looking at thumbnails with the naked eye will not reveal it.

### Q3. Keep the shot steady: product promo films by default add no handheld shake
Rule: bright-scene UI promo films forbid handheld shake; camera noise is used only in dark atmospheric films that explicitly pursue a documentary feel, at a very small value, and the rendered frames must be self-checked to confirm there is no unintended shaking.
Precedent: the user: "the camera angle is very poor, and there is a small shake and I don't know why". After removing the handheld shake and recomposing, there were zero complaints about shaking; another dark atmospheric film kept shake=0.02 and drew no complaint — this marks out the boundary of applicability (inferred boundary).
Self-check: look at the rendered frames shot by shot: is there any camera shake without a narrative purpose?

### Q4. Highlight / sweep effects: better absent than overused — no mass use, at most once per shot for the hero, and they must be clipped inside the rounded-corner boundary
Rule: glint / bloom-sweep type effects are not applied en masse — batch element entrances rely on the motion itself, not on each element lighting up in turn; any light effect that is kept must be clipped by the border-radius/overflow of the element that carries it, since light spilling past rounded corners is a typical source of a cheap look. The light-effect technique itself is not banned: a single-point sheen/bloom given to the hero and implemented to a high standard can be done; after doing it, look at the rendered frames and ask yourself "does it look good?" before handing it over.
Precedent: the user rejected it twice: "there is no need for every card to flash once", "cancel the bloom sweeping across when the card appears, it doesn't look good". Accordingly the per-card glint was removed, the bloom sweep was withdrawn, and the chip highlight sweep was clipped inside the rounded-corner boundary. Clarification (2026-07-13 user): the rejection was aimed at "the implemented version doesn't look good", and "does not mean this technique path / effect type is not viable" — the ban on mass use and the rounded-corner clipping are unchanged, but bloom/sweep-type techniques may be retried to a higher implementation standard.
Self-check: count the glint/sweep occurrences in the whole film: are there more than "once on the hero element"? Is each light effect clipped by the rounded corners? For each light effect that remains, have you watched it back at normal speed and found it good-looking?

### Q5. The opening gives only one hero: a single subject + a complete action arc beats a group dance
Rule: opening information should be sparse and focused — one hero element, one complete arc (spotlight → push-in → hover → edge light → settle); many elements dancing together cannot carry a first impression.
Precedent: the user: "the first shot only needs to focus on one card and make it have more finish". The opening was torn down six times in one night, and once the single-card structure was settled the structure was not touched again until the finished film.
Self-check: how many "things you want the audience to look at" does the opening shot have? Is the hero's action arc complete (start–development–landing)?

### Q6. Camera position serves readability: face information-dense shots straight on, text close-ups from the side horizontally, and verify stylised tilts shot by shot
Rule: stylised camera positions are decided shot by shot — narrative / emotional shots may tilt, while information-dense shots such as lists / stacks stay straight on; close-ups of cards containing text use a sideways horizontal camera position, avoiding steep low-angle / high-angle shots that damage text readability; applying one camera style across the board is forbidden.
Precedent: a uniform tilt across the whole film was rejected by the user: "it isn't done well... the change to the content list page (the paper list in the template film) is also bad, roll it back", and the list page was then separately reverted to straight on; the user: "focus on one card... shot from the left side rather than from below", changed to a left-side hero camera position.
Self-check: for each tilted-camera shot: what is the reason for the tilt? Can the text in the frame still be read at this angle?

### Q7. Object close-up four-piece set: side tilt angle + perceptible height + orbit + a contrasting dark material background
Rule: object / asset close-up shots should do the full environmental work — a side tilted camera position, a stack built up to a perceptible volume and height, the camera orbiting the subject, a dark material background that contrasts with the main scene (such as brushed metal), and then a synchronised pull-back transition returning to the main scene.
Precedent: the user: "the shot should be tilted, shot from the side, the stacked items should have some height, and then the camera rotates around the stack. The whole scene should be a dark metallic-textured background. And as the camera pulls back, the dashboard background appears at the same time". The pile close-up orbit went from the first version to the final version — dark brushed-metal background + a taller stack + orbiting camera move.
Self-check: does the close-up subject have a sense of volume and environmental contrast? Is the camera a locked-off position, or does it orbit / pull back?

### Q8. Make the ending a "launch-event group photo": elements from the whole film fly in from all sides to surround the wordmark, pushing energy to the film's peak
Rule: for a multi-feature product, the ending uses a "family portrait" structure — one representative element from each feature already shown flies in from multiple directions and freezes; the spec matches the film's energy peak (crane camera move + stage lighting + particles), and the first version of an ending is almost always too conservative, so add one notch by default.
Precedent: the user: "the core elements of each page fly in from different directions... forming a picture like a group photo", "add some more effects, animation, camera moves... like a product launch". It went from a group-photo-style assembly to launch-event level — crane camera move, stage lighting, gold dust.
Self-check: is the ending the highest energy point of the whole film? In the group photo, does every feature already shown have a representative element?

### Q9. The end point of a fly-in must be a real slot in the page layout; elements do not hover above the page
Rule: after an element flies in it must be "embedded" into the page (settle into a real grid slot, extend the scroll flow); landing and hovering above it looks fake.
Precedent: the user: "the list of research questions should fly in from the air and embed into the dashboard", "don't let the 10 project cards hover over it, instead keep scrolling down and then line up further down, without overlapping". Accordingly the row elements fly in and embed into the layout, and the grid was changed to extend with the scroll (a near-complete file rewrite).
Self-check: after each flying element settles, does it have real coordinates in the page layout? Is any element permanently floating above the page?

### Q10. Mock content in document-type shots must be publication-grade: native typesetting, text filled to the brim, the complete layout in frame
Rule: the audience reads every word on screen — mocks in document / report shots must be filled with a realistic density of fake content in the product's native typesetting, with layout elements such as the sidebar / comment column fully in frame; a "texture + slogan" level fake document means reworking the whole shot.
Precedent: the user: "the weekly report content text should use typesetting and style consistent with the current ones, only the content is mock, the text content filled to the brim, and the left sidebar and the right comment column should be shown completely" (in the template film this shot is the weekly report page). The first version, a "texture + slogan" level fake document, was redone as a whole shot, and the final version is a full native-typeset mock with both columns fully in frame.
Self-check: pause on any frame of the document shot: does the content read like the real thing? Is the layout (sidebar / comments / header) complete?

### Q11. Text meant to be read has a minimum effective text height: captions ≥5% of frame height, auxiliary text ≥3%; measure in final pixels, not by fontSize
Rule: calibrate the readability of a 1080p finished film for playback on a phone / embedded small window — shot-narration captions must have an **effective text height ≥56px (≥5.2% of frame height, the 60px tier recommended)**, and auxiliary text such as subtitles / statistics / URLs ≥32px (≥3%); effective text height = fontSize × all ancestor scales × perspective compression (for 3D shots estimate by `cos(rotY)`), and acceptance measures the actual pixels on the rendered frame, not the fontSize in the code. Text has only two states: **"texture"** (small decorative text in a screenshot cutout, which must be clearly blurred / dimmed so the audience does not try to read it) or **"to be read"** (a compliant size + enough contrast, with a scrim/drop shadow when over a light background); there is no in-between state of "re-laid-out but still unreadable" — text that cannot be read is better deleted. The closing URL / call to action is the last line in the whole film that should be small.
Precedent: the user (2026-07-25): "in several of the shots, you can see the text they carry is very small... the text in those shots is too small, you can't see it at all". Reviewing the gallery intro film: S1/S2/S4 captions at 38–40px (about 3.5% of frame height), the S6 sub-sentence at 30px, and the closing URL at 25px all fell below the line, and in S2 the panel subtitle that was "re-laid-out for readability" was given only 19px — a counter-example of the in-between state of re-laying out without reaching the standard; all six were raised uniformly to the 60/40/34px tiers and the rework passed.
Self-check: take one frame from each shot with captions and shrink it to 480px wide (simulating a phone small window): can each sentence still be read clearly? Measure the actual pixel height of the "to be read" text frame by frame: captions ≥56px, auxiliary ≥32px? Is any text in the in-between state of "meant to be read but unreadable"?

## Sound (S)

### S1. Choose music and sound effects by "film type", not by "event": a strong-kick electronic bed + a cinematic SFX vocabulary, ban game-sound-pack timbres (the ban is on timbre, not on the action)
Rule: the product promo SFX vocabulary = whoosh (camera move) / impact (landing) / riser (build-up) / sparkle (light effect) / transition (transition), and game-sound-pack timbres are banned; choose BGM with a strong kick and strong rhythm on an electronic bed (tech-house type), and candidate tracks must be laid into the finished film for an audition — listening to the track alone cannot tell you its character.
Precedent: the user: "this soundtrack and these sound effects are all too much like a game. Find me one with strong drum beats and a strong sense of rhythm, and the sound effects should be the typical sound effects of a product promo video". All the Kenney CC0 game sounds introduced earlier were replaced in one batch with Mixkit tech-house + whoosh/impact/riser/sparkle; the BGM went through three drafts within 34 minutes and the final one was kept.

**The ban is on timbre, not on the action** (easy to misread, be sure to distinguish): what this entry rules out is the **texture** of game sound packs — "synth pluck/bloop, cartoon bounce, game-over scale" — and not "no sound for clicks". If there really is a click / switch / shatter on screen, it should get that action's Foley — that is what S4 requires. The distinguishing question: does this sound like the **sound the real-world object makes** (shutter, mechanical switch, glass, paper), or like a **feedback prompt tone from a game engine**? Use the former, discard the latter.
- The template film itself uses `click-camera.mp3` and gives it the highest SFX loudness in the whole film, 0.6 — a real shutter sound, not a UI prompt tone.
- **`sfx/ui/` is not "the whole directory cleared"; it is precisely the category in the whole library that most needs auditioning file by file**: its 18 files include both real-object Foley (`switch-light` `switch-tap` `switch-click-quick`, whose Mixkit original name is literally light switch) and more than half synthetic UI feedback tones (`ui-confirm-bleep` `ui-confirm-tone` `ui-notify-tech` `ui-success-soft` `ui-tone-quick` `ui-popup-dry` `ui-message-pop` etc., original names tone/bleep/alert/notification) — **the latter are exactly the texture this entry excludes**, unless the film deliberately wants a "system prompt" narrative. See sound-design 3.3 for the list and the keep/drop calls.
- `sfx/glass/` is real shattering material sound (4 files) and is not on the banned list.

Self-check: listen to the audio track alone with eyes closed: does it sound like a product launch trailer, or like a mobile game? Was the BGM chosen after auditioning it in the finished film? For each UI-type sound effect, is it a real-object texture or a game feedback prompt tone?

### S2. Pin SFX beat by beat and manage them centrally; for rapid-fire sounds use two-sample alternation + a stepped volume decrease + accelerating intervals, and do not repeat at the same loudness
Rule: manage SFX in a declarative table of "frame number + audio source + volume", with each entry annotated with the on-screen action it corresponds to, ruling out laying sound effects by feel; when elements of the same kind fire in rapid succession, the machine-gun effect is prevented by three measures — alternating two samples, stepping the volume down along the sequence (such as six steps from 0.40→0.25), and letting the intervals follow the animation curve and accelerate; when it gets so dense that it smears into one mass, let the sound fade out into a swoosh (this repository has confirmed there is no playbackRate pitch-shifting, so the machine-gun effect is not prevented by pitch-shifting).
Precedent: the user: "the weekly report's left sidebar could use a different sound effect when each week appears, one that goes with the appearance of each week" (in the template film this is the weekly report page). First the beat-by-beat frame-pinned SFX frame-number table structure was set up (kept through to the finished film), then pluck two-sample alternation with the interval accelerating 8→3f was done, and finally the per-week pop six-shot burst volume was made into a 0.40→0.25 stepped decrease.
Self-check: is each SFX pinned to a specific frame number and annotated with its corresponding action? Does a rapid-fire passage sound like a "countable rhythm" or a mechanical repetition?

### S3. Do sound after the picture is locked; once the timeline moves, re-pin the whole SFX table
Rule: BGM/SFX are arranged after the picture structure is basically locked; any change that alters shot durations/order always ends with "re-align the frame numbers in the whole SFX table" — audio-picture misalignment is a must-check item before delivery.
Precedent: the user: "after making these changes, readjust the sound effect positions to match the new timeline". Accordingly the whole SFX table was re-pinned to the new timeline (total length 1085f); lesson chain: 11 hours after the first-version SFX were pinned, a big picture change forced a whole-table re-pin, and two of the three re-pins were purely the knock-on cost of picture rework.
Self-check: did any shot duration/order change this round? If so, was the SFX table re-aligned entry by entry?

### S4. Foley before decorative sound: the action on screen gets that action's real sound, and the audio is trimmed to be exactly as long as the action
Rule: whatever action happens on screen gets the matching sound (typing visuals get keyboard sounds, items dropping in one by one get a pop each), and a generic swoosh cannot cover a distinctive on-screen action; long samples are trimmed to the picture section (Sequence truncation), with duration exactly equal to the action.
Precedent: the user: "when the content of the weekly report page appears it could have a typing sound effect" (in the template film this is the weekly report page). Accordingly the document page reveal was given a typewriter sound, and keyboard.mp3 was trimmed to 24f/44f for two uses according to context.

Operating points (two special lists in the library, see sound-design 4.1): **long samples >5s (21 of them) must be given an explicit `durationInFrames`**, otherwise they drag past the end of the action; an impact with a long reverb tail is an exception — let the tail decay naturally. **Quiet-material files with peaks <-12dB (7 of them)**: `volume` is a multiplication factor, and even at 1.0 it just plays back as-is and is still covered by the BGM — the first choice is to swap the material or pre-normalise it, and if necessary give a gain >1 and verify the peak on the rendered output.

Self-check: does each distinctive on-screen action (typing / click / landing) have its own Foley? Does any sound effect drag on and keep sounding after the action ends? Is each sound effect actually audible in the finished film (is the volume of quiet material enough)?

### S5. Audio-picture alignment adds two offsets: the output audio track offset is measured per render pipeline and subtracted uniformly; the source's peak lag is measured per file
Rule: the whole audio track of a rendered film has a fixed lag relative to the video, with the root cause in the output encoding / muxing chain (AAC encoder priming, typically 2048 samples ≈1.28f@30fps at 48kHz; Remotion tracks this at remotion-dev/remotion#7099), not in the behaviour of `<Audio>`/`Sequence.from` — measure it per render pipeline (Remotion version + codec + sample rate + container) and record it (extract the audio track from the rendered film and take 2–3 sharp SFX original files for normalised cross-correlation; the measurement counts as accurate only if the residuals are consistent), and the compensation is subtracted uniformly in the `from` calculation expression at the render layer rather than editing frame numbers entry by entry; the source's own peak lag (the peak is not at the start of the file) is measured per file. The unified formula is `from = target peak frame − peak lag − output offset`; for the method and boundaries (truncated-type sound effect windows are not lengthened, the first 2f of the film cannot be fully compensated) see sound-design §4.6.
Precedent: AiflPromo measured click lag of 2032 samples and impact lag of 2066 samples (≈1.27–1.29f), consistent with the AAC priming fingerprint, and the rendered product's elst does not declare trimming of the padding at the head; in the promo-ink era 4f (≈133ms, far beyond the priming order of magnitude, root cause unverified) was measured — constants cannot be carried across pipelines. Without compensation the two items stack and the heaviest hit comes 0.27s late, clearly reading as audio-picture out of sync; in a beat-hit film a 2–3 frame difference is enough for reasonable beat-hit motion to be read as groundless shake (one of the causes of the 2026-08-22 user feedback).
Self-check: has the output audio track offset of this pipeline been measured (where are the Remotion version / codec / sample rate / container recorded along with the value)? Is the compensation subtracted uniformly in the `from` calculation expression, or scattered among individual frame numbers? For sources with a clear attack peak, has the peak lag been measured?

## Copy (C)

### C1. Rewrite the copy once against the final shots after the picture is locked, and give pure-animation sections narration too
Rule: the first-draft copy is only a placeholder — once the picture is finalised, rewrite it shot by shot against the finished film; pure-animation shots get a short narration caption, leaving no "mute sections" (except the brand closing shot, which stays clean).
Precedent: the user: "rewrite the copy wording based on my complete video content as it is now, I feel the current wording doesn't match the video picture very well, and also, should the animation parts get some narration too?". Accordingly the title copy was rewritten, and the live-action shots were given caption narration.
Self-check: compare sentence by sentence: does each line of copy match the picture it sits on? Is there any animation section longer than 3 seconds without narration?

### C2. Write slogans as "team + product feature name + concrete benefit", and make every abstract metaphorical word concrete
Rule: slogans should be concrete and carry the product feature name (Paper Radar / weekly report), avoiding abstract metaphorical words (generalised slogans of the board/place kind); add a lead-in title card before an important feature appears.
Precedent: the user edited the draft word by word: "change 'All your research, one board' to 'All team's research, one place to go'... change the 'The morning reading' line to 'Paper Radar, tailored for your morning reading'"; "before the final document page is shown, a text screen should also be added" (in the template film this is the weekly report page). Accordingly the three sentences were replaced word by word, and a lead-in title card for the document page was added.
Self-check: does each slogan contain a concrete feature name / benefit word? Is any sentence propped up only by a metaphor?

### C3. Annotation text in 3D shots lives in the same 3D space: page-space, same perspective, shot obliquely, large type + highlighter highlight
Rule: when adding in-frame annotations to the subject in a 3D scene, the text must enter the same 3D space (page coordinate system, the same camera perspective, translateZ hover + cast shadow); flat overlaid text is disconnected from the scene; the style uses large serif type + marker highlight on the key words.
Precedent: the user: "the text is on the left, make it larger and more eye-catching, with a highlight, and the text's viewing angle should also be 3D floating in the air, and shot obliquely" (redone after the flat title card of the first version was rejected). The hero-hover annotation was changed to be placed in 3D — viewed obliquely in the page coordinate system, with marker highlight.
Self-check: for each piece of text in a 3D shot: does it move with the scene camera, or is it stuck onto the screen glass?

## Process (P)

### P1. Before delivery, render and check frame screenshots one by one yourself; do not hand the first check to the user
Rule: after each round of changes, first render key-frame screenshots yourself and check them one by one (composition / shake / text sharpness / blooper), and where necessary prove it to yourself with pixel-level tools (crop and enlarge / two-frame diff) before handing to the user.
Precedent: the user, on first watching the film, demanded: "did you check the quality with screenshots yourself? The camera angle is very poor, and there is a small shake and I don't know why". From then on crop/diff/measure self-check scripts and an out/qa/ per-version still-frame archive grew in the pipeline, and both sides entered a frame-based review.
Self-check: before handing out this version, which frames have I looked at myself? Is there screenshot evidence for the key shots?

### P2. Break reference material down frame by frame into a list of techniques first, then judge suitability shot by shot, and do not apply it globally
Rule: when there is a reference film, first extract frames and do a motion breakdown (a list of how each animation / transition / effect is implemented) and write it into the spec, and make the call per technique on suitability for the project; image references describe the feel of "a certain kind of shot" and not a style directive for the whole film — before landing, judge shot by shot whether it applies, and self-check after changes before handing over.
Precedent: the user: "capture as many frames as possible from the original video... reproduce them and then adapt them to our project, don't force adaptation for what doesn't fit" — after the reference film's motion breakdown was written into the spec, approvals followed one after another; counter-example: three reference images were applied globally as a uniform tilted camera position, rejected with "it isn't done well... roll it back", and redone shot by shot.
Self-check: does every technique of the reference material have an explicit "adopt / discard" decision? Have I treated one reference image as a style directive for the whole film?

### P3. For feedback with an ambiguous referent, confirm the object first; when a change goes wrong, cleanly revert the whole commit and do not patch on the wrong version
Rule: for vague feedback such as "the first title", confirm what is meant before acting; if the wrong object was changed, immediately revert the whole commit and start again; keep each change as an independent commit so it can be rolled back cleanly.
Precedent: the user: "it's not the first title card, I mean the animation where AI field lab appears at the opening and the end". It was first misunderstood and card1 was held, then cleanly reverted within 8 minutes, and redone on the correct object, which passed.
Self-check: am I sure of the referent of this feedback? If something goes wrong at the current commit granularity, can it be rolled back in one step?

### P4. List the feature inventory first and map each item to a shot; one animation technique is the hero only once in the whole film, and repeated shots/information are always deleted
Rule: before shooting, list the product feature inventory (dashboard / detail / papers / weekly report / brand) and map each item to a shot — missing a core feature means rework; one technique (fly-in / stacking / page-turn) is the hero only once in the whole film; the editing self-check filters by "what new information does this shot give", the same material / information appears only once in the whole film, and repetition is deleted.
Precedent: the user: "the other animations are somewhat homogeneous. They don't fully show the features of the whole website", "very good, but is the weekly report part not shown?" (in the template film this is the weekly report page) — feature shots were added accordingly; "the second-to-last shot is a repeat of the project cards shot, it can be removed" — accordingly the repeated overview shot was deleted and the closing tagline de-duplicated.
Self-check: does every item in the feature inventory have a corresponding shot? Are two shots using the same technique as the hero, or telling the same thing?
